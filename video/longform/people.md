## Cold open

> SCENE (traffic): A street lined with parked cars. A ball has rolled out between two of them; a child stands at the kerb behind.
> pe03

[curious] Picture a quiet residential street on a Saturday afternoon. Cars parked along the kerb. You're driving at a perfectly legal speed. Then a ball bounces out between two parked cars.

[pause] What happens in the next second?

[warm] You probably already know. And if you know it, your foot is on the brake before you see anything else. That's this whole lesson in one moment. Driving isn't only about cars. It's about the people with no metal box around them.

> SCENE (title): Title card.
> Rücksicht

[serious] The very first rule of the German road traffic regulations asks two things of everyone: constant care, and [[gegenseitige Rücksicht]], mutual consideration. Today, that means pedestrians, children, cyclists, buses, trams and trains. Twenty questions, and seven of them are worth five points.

## People on foot

> SCENE (sign): The zebra crossing sign.
> 350

[curious] Let's start with the zebra crossing, the [[Zebrastreifen]]. Its proper name is [[Fußgängerüberweg]], and the blue sign marks it. Here, people on foot come first.

{Qpe01}

> SCENE (question): Question pe01. A pedestrian waits at the zebra crossing.

[curious] A pedestrian is standing at a zebra crossing, waiting. They haven't stepped onto it yet. What do you do?

[countdown] Stop and let them cross. Someone who clearly wants to cross already has priority, on foot or in a wheelchair. You don't wait for the first step. You approach at a moderate speed, ready to stop. And the horn is for warning of danger, not for announcing that you're coming through.

{Qpe12}

> SCENE (question): Question pe12. A pedestrian crossing the road away from any crossing.

[firm] Now a harder one, because this time the pedestrian is in the wrong. Someone is crossing the road, nowhere near a crossing. What do you do?

[countdown] Brake and let them cross, even if they should really have waited. Yes, pedestrians have to look out for traffic. But the first rule of the StVO says nobody may be endangered. Someone else's mistake never gives you the right to hit them.

{Qpe05}

> SCENE (question): Question pe05. An older pedestrian, still crossing, as the light turns green.

[warm] Your light turns green. But an older pedestrian is still crossing in front of you, slowly. What do you do?

[countdown] Wait until they have left the road. Green means you may go. It never means you may endanger anyone. Older people often need longer, and an engine revving or a horn can startle them. Let them finish crossing in peace. It's ten seconds of your day.

{Qpe04}

> SCENE (question): Question pe04. A pedestrian with a white cane.

[curious] A pedestrian with a white cane wants to cross the road. What does that mean?

[countdown] They are blind or severely visually impaired. And I must be especially careful and stop if necessary. Two right answers. A white cane, a guide dog in a white harness, or a yellow armband with three black dots: all three tell you the same thing.

[serious] This person may not see you at all. No eye contact, no wave across the road. They're listening to the traffic, and trusting it. So stop, wait, and let them cross. Make that trust the right call.

{Qpe09}

> SCENE (question): Question pe09. Night. A pedestrian in dark clothes at the edge of the road.

[curious] At night, pedestrians in dark clothes are very hard to see. What is true?

[countdown] With dipped headlights I often see them very late. And I match my speed to the reach of my lights. On dipped beam, someone in dark clothes often appears only twenty-five to thirty metres ahead. At fifty, that's less than your stopping distance. And daytime running lights don't light the road at all, and leave the back of your car dark.

[warm] And if you walk along a country road at night yourself: wear something light. You're much harder to see than you think.

## Children

> SCENE (sign): The children warning sign.
> 136

[warm] Children aren't small adults. They can't judge how fast a car is coming, or how far away it is. And when something catches their attention, the road simply disappears. So the law asks more of you, not of them.

{Qpe02}

> SCENE (question): Question pe02. Two children at the roadside.

[curious] There are children at the roadside. How do you behave?

[countdown] Slow down and be ready to brake. And expect a child to run into the road suddenly. Both. The same goes near older people, and anyone who needs help: slow down, foot ready over the brake, so that they can't come to harm. The horn won't stop a child. Your brakes will.

{Qpe03}

> SCENE (question): Question pe03. The ball between the parked cars.

[curious] Back to the start. A ball rolls into the road between parked cars. What do you expect?

[countdown] That a child will run out after it. A child chasing a ball sees only the ball. Not you, not the car, not the kerb. So you brake the moment the ball appears. Ball means child.

[dry amusement] Your paintwork will survive the ball. Think about who's coming after it.

{Qpe06}

> SCENE (question): Question pe06. Children on bicycles.

[curious] Children on bikes. Up to what age must they ride on the pavement?

[countdown] 8. Until their eighth birthday, children must ride on the pavement. Until their tenth, they may. So at every driveway and every side street, expect a small cyclist to roll off the kerb and into the road, often without looking.

## Cyclists and e-scooters

> SCENE (sign): The cycle path signs.
> 237 | 240

[curious] Cyclists are traffic, just like you, only without a crumple zone. When you overtake them, you need at least one and a half metres in town, and two outside. And they ride the way the road lets them: round potholes, round puddles, round parked cars.

{Qpe07}

> SCENE (question): Question pe07. A cyclist ahead, coming up to a parked car.

[curious] A cyclist ahead of you is coming up to a parked car. What do you expect?

[countdown] That they will swerve left, perhaps without signalling. They have to get round that car somehow, and not everyone looks back first. So you hang back, keep your distance, and only overtake once it's clear what they're going to do.

{Qpe08}

> SCENE (question): Question pe08. Two cyclists side by side.

[firm] And the one that makes some drivers furious. May cyclists ride side by side?

[countdown] Yes, as long as they do not hold up traffic. Two abreast is fine, as long as nobody's held up. And on a cycle street, or in a group of sixteen or more, they may ride side by side even if it slows you down.

[dry amusement] So the cycling club two by two on a Sunday morning isn't being rude. It's following the StVO.

{Qpe10}

> SCENE (question): Question pe10. E-scooters.

[curious] E-scooters. Where and how may they be ridden?

[countdown] On cycle paths, and on the road where there is none. And with one person only, aged fourteen or over. The pavement is off limits. And when you turn, treat an e-scooter exactly like a bicycle: same shoulder check, same priority.

## Buses and trams

> SCENE (sign): The bus and tram stop sign.
> 224

[curious] Now, the big vehicles that stop for people: buses and trams. The rules here all protect the same moment, when passengers are on foot, right next to the road, and often in a hurry.

{Qpe11}

> SCENE (question): Question pe11. A school bus at the roadside, children getting off.

[warm] A school bus has stopped at the roadside, and children are getting off. What do you do?

[countdown] Pass slowly, with plenty of room, ready to brake. And expect children to run across in front of or behind the bus. A child who's just got off the bus is thinking about home, or a friend across the road. Not about you. And if the bus has its hazard lights on, it gets stricter still.

{Qra04}

> SCENE (question): Question ra04. A bus approaching a stop, hazard lights flashing.

[firm] A bus is heading for its stop with the hazard lights flashing. What applies?

[countdown] I must not overtake it. A bus or school bus approaching a stop with its hazards on may not be overtaken. You don't have to stop dead. But racing past before it stops is exactly what this rule is there to prevent.

{Qra05}

> SCENE (question): Question ra05. The bus at its stop, hazards on; the blue car comes the other way.

[curious] Now the bus is standing at its stop, hazard lights on, and you're coming the other way. How may you pass it?

[countdown] Only at walking pace. And without endangering passengers, waiting if necessary. [[Schrittgeschwindigkeit]] means the speed of someone walking. Not thirty. And it applies to oncoming traffic too, on the same carriageway. A child running round the front of the bus won't check which way you're coming from.

{Qra06}

> SCENE (question): Question ra06. A bus signalling left to pull away from its stop.

[curious] A bus is signalling left, ready to pull away from its stop. What do you do?

[countdown] Let it pull out, waiting if necessary. A bus leaving a marked stop must be allowed to go, and everyone else waits.

[dry amusement] Hurrying past doesn't save you anything. You'll only meet it again at the next stop.

{Qra07}

> SCENE (question): Question ra07. A tram at a stop with no island; passengers step off into the road.

[serious] Trams are trickier. Many tram stops have no island, so the passengers step straight off the tram and into your lane. The tram has stopped, and people are getting off. What do you do?

[countdown] Pass on the right at walking pace without hindering passengers. And wait if necessary. With people crossing your lane, that often just means waiting until the road is clear. Passing quickly on the left, along the tracks, is never the answer.

## Level crossings

> SCENE (sign): The level-crossing signs.
> 151 | 201

[curious] And finally, the one vehicle that never gives way: the train. The [[Andreaskreuz]], the St Andrew's cross, marks a level crossing, and trains always have priority there. Before it, a warning sign and the striped countdown markers tell you it's coming.

{Qra08}

> SCENE (question): Question ra08. The level-crossing warning sign and the St Andrew's cross.

[curious] You're approaching a level crossing marked with a St Andrew's cross. How do you approach?

[countdown] At a moderate speed. Slow enough that you could stop if a train comes. And from the warning sign on, you may not overtake motor vehicles. So the lorry in front of you stays in front.

{Qra01}

> SCENE (question): Question ra01. A level crossing with the St Andrew's cross.

[firm] When must you wait at this level crossing?

[countdown] When a train is approaching. And when red lights are flashing or the barriers are coming down. Also when a railway worker signals you to stop. And here's the trap: a barrier that's coming down is already closed. You don't wait for it to finish.

{Qra02}

> SCENE (question): Question ra02. A queue just beyond the tracks.

[curious] Traffic is queuing just beyond the level crossing. What do you do?

[countdown] Wait before the level crossing. You only drive onto a crossing if you can get all the way across without stopping. Stuck on the tracks in a queue, with cars in front and behind, you have nowhere to go.

{Qra03}

> SCENE (question): Question ra03. A car broken down on the tracks.

[serious] And the moment nobody wants. Your car breaks down on a level crossing. What do you do?

[countdown] Get everyone out at once and away from the danger zone. Then call the emergency number or the number shown at the crossing. A train can't swerve, and it often needs more than a kilometre to stop. So: people first, then the phone. The car can be replaced. You can't.

## What to take with you

> SCENE (list): The four ideas of this lesson.

> Ball heißt Kind | a ball means a child @ ball

[warm] So, that's the people you share the road with. Care beats being right. A ball means a child. A bus at its stop with its hazards on means walking pace, from both directions. And the train always goes first.
