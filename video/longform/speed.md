## Cold open

> SCENE (sign): The warning sign for children.
> 136

[curious] Picture a quiet street, cars parked along both sides. A ball rolls out between two of them. You know what usually comes next: a child, running after it.

[serious] Two drivers see it at the same moment. One is doing thirty, the other fifty. Only twenty kilometres an hour apart. That doesn't sound like much.

> SCENE (stat): Stopping distance by the rule of thumb.
> 18 | metres to stop from 30 km/h @ eighteen
> 40 | metres to stop from 50 km/h @ forty

[firm] But the first driver stops in about eighteen metres. The second needs about forty. More than twice as far. And in this lesson, you'll learn to work out numbers like these in your head, in a few seconds.

> SCENE (title): Title card.
> Tempo & Anhalteweg

[warm] This is speed and stopping distance. The test loves these questions, because many of them have a number for an answer. Don't worry. The maths is four rules of thumb, and none of them is harder than the times tables.

## The numbers on the signs

> SCENE (sign): The town sign, front and back.
> 310 | 311

[curious] Let's start with the limits that apply when no sign gives you one. They hang on a single yellow sign: the [[Ortstafel]], the town sign. From the front, it means you're in town, and the limit is fifty. From the back, with the name crossed out, you've left town.

{Qsp01}

> SCENE (question): Question sp01. The speed limit outside town.

[curious] So once you're past that sign, on a road that isn't a motorway, what's the limit for a car?

[countdown] 100 kilometres an hour. On every country road, unless a sign says otherwise. And it's a ceiling, not a target.

{Qsp02}

> SCENE (question): Question sp02. A motorway with no speed limit shown.

[firm] And now the famous one. On a German motorway with no speed limit shown, what applies?

[countdown] A maximum of 130 km/h is recommended. It's a recommendation, the [[Richtgeschwindigkeit]], not a limit. But here's the catch. If you drive faster and you're involved in a crash, you can be held partly liable, even if you didn't cause it.

[dry amusement] And plenty of stretches do have a limit on a sign. Where they do, the sign wins, not the legend of the endless Autobahn.

> SCENE (stat): The three numbers to know.
> 50 | in town @ town
> 100 | outside town @ outside
> 130 | motorway, recommended @ motorway

[warm] So: in town, fifty. Outside town, a hundred. And on the motorway, one hundred and thirty, as a recommendation.

{Qsp03}

> SCENE (question): Question sp03. A car towing a trailer.

[curious] Now hitch a trailer to your car. What's the top speed outside towns and on the motorway, without any special approval?

[countdown] 80 kilometres an hour. Outside town and on the motorway alike. With a special "Tempo 100" approval for the car and trailer together, you may do a hundred on motorways and expressways.

## Slower than the sign

{Qsp15}

> SCENE (question): Question sp15. When the limit is too fast.

[curious] A sign shows you the most you may drive. So, when must you drive slower than it allows?

[countdown] In fog, rain or on slippery roads. And when there are children at the roadside. Your speed has to suit the road, the traffic, the weather and the people around you. Someone tailgating you, though, is no reason to slow down, and no reason to speed up either.

{Qsp04}

> SCENE (question): Question sp04. Thick fog on a country road; a car ahead, barely visible.

[curious] Fog, snow or rain, and you can see less than fifty metres. What's the most you may drive at?

[countdown] 50 kilometres an hour, and often less. Here's how to judge fifty metres: the white marker posts at the roadside, the [[Leitpfosten]], stand fifty metres apart. If you can't see the next one, you're below fifty metres of visibility.

{Qsp05}

> SCENE (question): Question sp05. Snow chains on the tyres.

[curious] Snow chains on. What's your top speed?

[countdown] 50 kilometres an hour, even where more would be allowed. And chains only go on when there's a continuous layer of snow.

{Qsp06}

> SCENE (question): Question sp06. A bend at night; the headlights reach only so far.

[firm] A bend at night. You can't see far ahead. How fast may you drive?

[countdown] So that I can stop within the distance I can see. And on very narrow roads, so that I can stop within half that distance. Why half? Because someone may be coming the other way, and they need room to stop too.

> SCENE (term): The rule behind it.
> Fahren auf Sicht | drive within your sight | StVO § 3

[warm] Driving schools call it [[Fahren auf Sicht]]. Whatever the sign allows, you only drive as fast as your eyes reach.

{Qsp16}

> SCENE (question): Question sp16. Driving very slowly.

[curious] And the other end of the scale. May you drive very slowly, for no reason at all?

[countdown] No, not so slowly that you hold up the flow of traffic. Slow isn't automatically safe. A car crawling along for no reason tempts the drivers behind it into risky overtaking.

## Reaction distance

> SCENE (term): The first rule of thumb.
> Reaktionsweg | speed ÷ 10, times 3 | rule of thumb

[curious] Now, the maths. It starts with the [[Reaktionsweg]], the reaction distance. That's how far you travel between seeing the danger and touching the brake. About one second, during which the car doesn't slow down at all.

[warm] The rule of thumb: take your speed, drop the zero, and multiply by three. That's it.

{Qsp18}

> SCENE (question): Question sp18. Reaction distance at 100 km/h.

[curious] Let's try it. At a hundred, how long is the reaction distance?

[countdown] 30 metres. A hundred, drop the zero: ten. Times three: thirty. That's thirty metres, about seven car lengths, before your foot even reaches the pedal.

{Qsp07}

> SCENE (question): Question sp07. Reaction distance at 50 km/h.

[curious] And at fifty?

[countdown] 15 metres. Five, times three. Fifteen metres of road, before anything happens. Remember that number. It comes back at the end of this lesson.

## Braking distance

> SCENE (term): The second rule of thumb.
> Bremsweg | (speed ÷ 10) × (speed ÷ 10) | rule of thumb

[curious] Then comes the [[Bremsweg]], the braking distance: from the moment you brake until the car stands still. The rule: drop the zero, and multiply the number by itself.

[dry amusement] If your times tables are rusty, relax. The test sticks to round speeds.

{Qsp08}

> SCENE (question): Question sp08. Normal braking distance at 50 km/h.

[curious] At fifty, with normal braking. How long is the braking distance?

[countdown] 25 metres. Five times five.

{Qsp19}

> SCENE (question): Question sp19. Normal braking distance at 30 km/h.

[curious] And at thirty?

[countdown] 9 metres. Three times three. From fifty it's twenty-five. That difference is why you see thirty outside schools. And notice: at thirty, reacting and braking both take nine metres. Above thirty, braking always takes more road than reacting.

{Qsp12}

> SCENE (question): Question sp12. Twice the speed.

[firm] Now a question that checks whether you've really understood it. You drive twice as fast. What happens to the reaction and braking distances?

[countdown] The reaction distance doubles. And the braking distance quadruples. Reaction grows in step with your speed. Braking grows with its square. Look at thirty and sixty: braking goes from nine metres to thirty-six. Four times as far.

[dry amusement] Physics doesn't negotiate. Twice the speed, four times the braking.

## Stopping distance

> SCENE (term): Both together.
> Anhalteweg | reaction + braking

[curious] Add the two together, and you get the [[Anhalteweg]], the stopping distance. Everything from seeing the danger to standing still.

> SCENE (stat): The stopping distance at 50 km/h.
> 15 | reaction @ fifteen
> 25 | braking @ twenty-five
> 40 | stopping, at 50 km/h @ forty

[warm] Remember the forty metres from the start? Now you know where they come from. Fifteen metres of reaction, twenty-five of braking. Forty to stop.

{Qsp09}

> SCENE (question): Question sp09. Stopping distance at 100 km/h.

[curious] Your turn. At a hundred, with normal braking, what's the stopping distance?

[countdown] 130 metres. Thirty of reaction, plus a hundred of braking. More than a football pitch.

{Qsp11}

> SCENE (question): Question sp11. Stopping distance at 70 km/h.

[curious] And at seventy?

[countdown] 70 metres. Seven times three is twenty-one. Seven times seven is forty-nine. Together, seventy.

[dry amusement] It's one of the few times the answer is the same as the question.

## Emergency stop

> SCENE (term): The third rule of thumb.
> Gefahrbremsung | emergency stop: half the braking distance | rule of thumb

[curious] One more rule. In a [[Gefahrbremsung]], an emergency stop, you brake as hard as the car can. And the braking distance halves. Only the braking part, though. Your reaction takes just as long as before.

{Qsp10}

> SCENE (question): Question sp10. Emergency stop from 100 km/h.

[curious] An emergency stop from a hundred. How long is the braking distance?

[countdown] 50 metres. Normal braking would be a hundred. Half of that: fifty. In the test, read the question carefully: "normal braking" or "emergency stop". That one phrase halves the answer.

{Qsp17}

> SCENE (question): Question sp17. Emergency stop from 80 km/h.

[curious] And from eighty?

[countdown] 32 metres. Eight times eight is sixty-four. Half of that, thirty-two. But the twenty-four metres of reaction at eighty don't shrink at all. Those you still travel at full speed.

{Qsp14}

> SCENE (question): Question sp14. What stretches the stopping distance.

[curious] These rules of thumb assume an alert driver and a dry road. So what makes the stopping distance longer?

[countdown] Tiredness, because the reaction comes later. And a wet or slippery road. Tiredness, distraction or alcohol stretch the reaction. Rain, leaves, snow, worn tyres or a heavy load stretch the braking. New tyres with deep tread do the opposite: they help you stop.

## Keeping your distance

{Qsp20}

> SCENE (question): Question sp20. Following a car at 50 km/h in town.

[curious] All of this decides how far behind the next car you should be. You're doing fifty in town. What's the minimum sensible gap?

[countdown] About the distance I cover in one second, around 15 metres. Sound familiar? It's your reaction distance. If the car ahead brakes hard, you need at least that much, just to get your foot onto the brake. One car length is far too little. And staying close so that nobody cuts in gets it the wrong way round. If someone does cut in, you drop back again.

> SCENE (term): The rule outside town.
> halber Tacho | half the speedometer, in metres | StVO § 4

[warm] Outside town, the rule is [[halber Tacho]], half the speedometer. Half your speed, in metres. It works out at just under two seconds. To check it, count: [[einundzwanzig, zweiundzwanzig]].

{Qsp13}

> SCENE (question): Question sp13. Following a car at 80 km/h outside town.

[curious] Last one. You're doing eighty outside town. What gap should you keep, at least?

[countdown] 40 metres. Half of eighty. And if you're much closer than that, under three tenths of it, that's no longer careless. It's tailgating, and it's fined.

## What to take with you

> SCENE (list): The four rules of thumb.
> Reaktionsweg | speed ÷ 10 × 3 @ reaction
> Bremsweg | (speed ÷ 10)² @ braking
> Gefahrbremsung | half the braking @ emergency
> Halber Tacho | the gap outside town @ half

[warm] So, four rules of thumb. Reaction: drop the zero, times three. Braking: drop the zero, times itself. Emergency stop: half the braking. And outside town, keep half the speedometer in metres.
